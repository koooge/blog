In September 2026, I passed course D of SFI (Svenska för invandrare, or Swedish for immigrants) and graduated from SFI.
I moved to Sweden in 2019, so it has been almost seven years. That doesn't mean I was studying Swedish seriously from day one, though. Here's a look back at learning Swedish alongside work and parenting.

## English came first
When I first moved here, I prioritized English over Swedish. I had never studied abroad, and this was my first time living overseas. My TOEIC score was just under 800 at the time. My English still needed work, so I figured I'd start there.
I did use Duolingo for Swedish from shortly after moving. It was useful for learning basic vocabulary and grammar.
So I wasn't attending SFI all those years. At first, I focused on English and picked up a little Swedish through the app. There's quite a gap between how long I've lived here and how long I've been studying Swedish seriously.

## Starting SFI
As the pandemic eased, I started thinking about studying Swedish for everyday life.
SFI has four courses, A through D, with D being the final course. Not everyone starts at A; the starting point depends on things like educational background and existing knowledge of Swedish. I started with C and then moved on to D.
A remote course became available, so I enrolled in course C. I started studying seriously in October 2024.
Classes were twice a week, about an hour and a half each, on Microsoft Teams. I attended while working. We also spent some time talking with classmates using Teams.
Even remotely, we weren't just listening to the teacher. We had time to talk to each other. Alongside learning vocabulary and grammar on Duolingo, I was now getting some conversation practice in class.

After I started SFI, we had a child, and I continued studying alongside work and parenting.
People living in Sweden generally speak good English, but some only speak Swedish. At förskola (preschool), in particular, many teachers don't speak English, so Swedish is needed to communicate with them. These days, I do my best to talk with the teachers in my still-halting Swedish. After having a child, these everyday situations gave me another reason to keep studying.

## Listening is hard
Of the four skills—reading, listening, writing, and speaking—listening was the hardest for me. I struggled with it in the exams too.
Swedish has nine vowel letters: a, e, i, o, u, y, å, ä, and ö. Japanese has five vowels, and many of the Swedish sounds seem similar to my ears. Telling them apart was difficult. Short words are especially hard to distinguish.
Around the time I started course C, I began practicing listening with SR's "Radio Sweden på lätt svenska" (radio news in easy Swedish) and "SVT Nyheter" (SVT news). I'd sit at my desk, watch or listen online, and look up unfamiliar words in a dictionary. Even with that practice, listening remained difficult through the end of the course.
Rather than leaving the news on in the background, I studied at my desk and worked through the content. It was listening practice, but looking up words and checking the text meant reading was part of it too.

## I used AI too
AI was developing rapidly while I was studying, so I also used ChatGPT and Gemini.
I'd ask them to translate news articles and other texts into English and Japanese, then use the translations to check the structure of the original Swedish sentences.
Swedish has similarities to English, so I sometimes found the English translation more useful than the Japanese one. I'd check the meaning in Japanese and compare the English translation with the Swedish original.
Understanding the general meaning in Japanese is one thing; understanding how the original sentence is put together is another. Having the English translation alongside it made the Swedish easier to follow. I had prioritized English when I first moved here, and ended up using it to study Swedish too.

## Graduating after about two years
I passed the course C exam in May 2025 and course D in September 2026. About two years after getting serious about studying, I finally graduated from SFI, alongside work and parenting.
Looking back, I gradually added more tools: Duolingo, classes, news, and AI. Learning vocabulary and grammar, talking with classmates, and listening to the news and looking things up all gave me different ways to practice Swedish.

## Next up: SVA grund
These days, I try to use Swedish for simple interactions, like ordering at a shop. Little by little, I'm using what I've studied in everyday life.
Next, I plan to move on to SVA grund (basic-level Swedish as a second language). I'll keep studying and practicing Swedish in daily life.
